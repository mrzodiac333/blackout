Shelter: Stay Dry, Stay Warm

How to shelter in place, seal a room, and build an emergency shelter outdoors that keeps you dry and warm.

Survival Last reviewed 2026-10-19 8 min read

Exposure kills faster than hunger. Shelter is about the microclimate next to your body: dry, wind-blocked, insulated from the ground. In most urban emergencies, the best shelter is your home with the utilities managed safely.

Sheltering in place

  • Storm/cyclone: interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows; lie under a sturdy table if debris risk is high.
  • Flood: move to higher floors, not the attic unless you can reach the roof. Never walk or drive through moving water.
  • Earthquake: drop, cover, hold on. Stay away from glass and heavy furniture.
  • Chemical/airborne hazard: interior room, windows and doors closed, vents and gaps taped, plastic sheeting over openings. Turn off AC and fans that draw outside air.
  • Extreme heat: lowest floor, shaded side, cross-ventilation at night; close curtains by day.
  • Extreme cold: smallest room, close off unused rooms, layer clothing, use safe heating only.

Sealing a room

  1. Choose a room with few windows and an interior wall; bring water, food, radio, medications, buckets and bedding.
  2. Close and lock windows and doors; seal gaps with tape or damp towels.
  3. Tape plastic sheeting over windows and vents; overlap edges.
  4. Turn off fans, AC, and any system that pulls in outside air.
  5. Listen to the radio or official alerts for the all-clear.

Sealing is temporary. It buys hours, not days, and can cause heat build-up in hot climates — monitor occupants.

Emergency shelter outdoors

  • Site: high, dry ground; clear of dead trees, rockfall, flood paths and animal trails.
  • Insulation first: a thick layer of dry leaves, pine needles or cardboard between you and the ground.
  • Wind and rain: orient the opening away from prevailing wind; use a tarp or branches layered like shingles.
  • Size: just big enough to lie down — smaller shelters trap heat.
  • Ventilation: never seal a shelter with a fire inside.
Urban alternativeIn a city, a parked car, a stairwell or a corridor can be a better shelter than an improvised outdoor structure. Prefer solid buildings over tents in high winds and civil unrest.

Cold, wet and heat

Hypothermia

Watch for shivering, confusion, slurred speech and drowsiness. Move the person to shelter, remove wet clothing, insulate from the ground, wrap in dry layers and share body heat. Warm the core, not the limbs. Give warm sweet drinks only if fully conscious.

Heat illness

Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea. Move to shade, loosen clothing, cool with wet cloths, sip water or ORS. Heat stroke: hot dry skin, confusion, collapse — a medical emergency; cool aggressively and call for help.
