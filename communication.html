Communication & Radio

Build a communications plan, choose legal gear, and understand Indian radio licensing — including what you can build and what you must not transmit without a licence.

Communication Last reviewed 2026-10-19 12 min read

When networks fail, radio still works. But in India, transmitting without a licence is illegal except on specific low-power, type-approved devices. This page separates what you can legally do today from what requires training and a licence.

Legal first

Listening is generally unrestricted. Transmitting on amateur, business or public-safety bands requires a WPC licence. Only WPC-approved low-power 446 MHz devices are licence-free. Never use jammers, amplifiers or modified export radios.

A communication plan

  • Primary: mobile phones with saved contacts and offline maps.
  • Secondary: SMS — texts often get through when calls fail.
  • Tertiary: FM/AM radio for official information (All India Radio).
  • Local: licence-free handhelds for household or neighbourhood use within a few hundred metres to a few kilometres.
  • Long range: amateur radio with a licensed operator.
  • Out-of-area contact: someone outside your region who can relay messages. Everyone in the family calls them.
Check-in disciplineAgree a daily check-in time and a backup time. Keep messages short: who, where, status, needs. Write a simple message form and practise it.

India licensing primer (WPC)

The Wireless Planning and Coordination Wing (WPC) of the Department of Telecommunications regulates all radio transmission in India.

Amateur radio grades

Indian amateur licence grades. Verify current fees and power limits with WPC — rules change.
GradeCall signExamNotes
RestrictedVU3ASOC written (radio theory + regulations)No Morse requirement; lower power limits
GeneralVU2ASOC written + 8 wpm Morse testHigher power; broader privileges
  • Applications and exams are handled through WPC and the SaralSanchar portal; local ham clubs help with scheduling and training.
  • Fees have historically been around ₹1,000 for 20 years or ₹2,000 for a lifetime licence — confirm current amounts.
  • Minimum age and citizenship rules apply; clubs and institutions can hold station licences.
  • Your licence carries your call sign and station location. Portable operation rules have relaxed, but restricted areas remain off-limits.

Licence-free devices

  • Only low-power 446 MHz handhelds with WPC Equipment Type Approval (ETA) are licence-free.
  • Since 2025, e-commerce listings must disclose frequency and ETA status. If a listing does not, treat it as suspect.
  • FRS, GMRS, MURS and many imported "walkie-talkies" are not legal to transmit with in India.
  • Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and similar low-power devices are covered by separate exemption rules.
Prohibited

Jammers, signal boosters, modified radios, and transmitters on public-safety or aviation frequencies are illegal and dangerous. Do not buy, build or use them.

Gear tiers

Tier 0 — receive only (no licence)

  • Battery/hand-crank AM/FM radio for All India Radio and IMD warnings.
  • An RTL-SDR dongle with an antenna lets you listen to a wide range of signals from a laptop. Receiving is legal; do not transmit.
  • A crystal radio is a zero-power receive-only project — see below.

Tier 1 — licence-free local

  • A pair of WPC-approved 446 MHz handhelds for your household or neighbourhood group.
  • Spare rechargeable batteries, a charging plan, and a printed channel plan.

Tier 2 — licensed amateur

  • VHF/UHF handheld or mobile after passing ASOC Restricted Grade.
  • HF transceiver and a wire antenna after General Grade.
  • Licensed kit builds such as a Pixie 40 m CW transceiver or uBITX SSB kit (advanced).

What you can build

Building receivers and licensed transceiver kits is legal. Transmitting still requires a licence and must stay within your privileges.

Crystal radio (receive only)

Kit transceivers

  • Pixie 40 m CW kit: low-cost, low-power Morse transceiver. Good for learning; requires a licensed operator to transmit.
  • uBITX SSB transceiver: a more advanced multi-band kit for experienced builders.
  • Antennas: build your own dipole, ground plane, J-pole or Yagi — see antenna plans.
RF safety

Keep antennas away from people when transmitting, never touch an antenna while transmitting, and keep all antennas clear of power lines. RF exposure and electrocution are real hazards.

Voice procedure

  • Listen first. Do not interrupt an emergency net.
  • Identify with your call sign on licensed bands.
  • Be brief: who you are, where you are, what you need.
  • Use plain language — avoid codes unless your group has trained on them.
  • Spell difficult words with a phonetic alphabet (Alpha, Bravo, Charlie…).
  • Confirm critical messages by repeating them back.
  • Priority: distress, urgency, safety, then routine. Never transmit false distress calls.

Example message

THIS IS VU3ABC, VU3ABC.
LOCATION: KALYAN NAGAR, BLOCK C.
STATUS: FOUR ADULTS, ONE CHILD, SAFE, WATER LOW.
NEED: DRINKING WATER, 20 LITRES.
WILL MONITOR 145.500 SIMPLEX AT 18:00.
OVER.

Emergency communication

  • In a disaster, amateur radio operators often support official relief. The Amateur Radio Society of India (ARSI) and local clubs coordinate nets.
  • If you are unlicensed, do not transmit on amateur bands — even in an emergency, use official channels and licensed operators.
  • Use SMS and messaging apps where data works; keep messages short and battery use low.
  • Write down information you receive: time, source, message. Rely on official sources, not rumours.
  • Keep a paper list of frequencies and contacts; do not rely on a phone that may die.
